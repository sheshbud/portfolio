Featured: Adaptive Wi-Fi Resilience for Hospital Networks

Medtronic Product Security · Summer 2026 · Hardware + embedded systems research

Connected medical devices are validated against interference in clean, controlled labs, but real hospitals are noisier and nothing keeps a device adapting once it's out in the field. This project asks three questions: what happens when other RF shows up, can it disrupt the link, and can a system catch that and re-route on its own? The result auto-senses trouble and re-routes itself, live, with zero manual input.

What I built

Specced, wired, and assembled the entire testbed from scratch: a Raspberry Pi 5 as the access point, an ESP32 client reporting live metrics, additional ESP32s acting as interference generators, and an ALFA adapter independently monitoring all nearby Wi-Fi to separate real traffic from noise. On top of that hardware, I wrote a decision engine from scratch that fuses latency, jitter, packet loss, and signal strength into a single health score, with two safety mechanisms layered on top: a trend check that catches degradation early before the score even crosses "bad," and a guard that only switches channel or band if the alternative is genuinely better, reverting otherwise so it doesn't overreact to normal RF noise.

Testing approach

One variable at a time (baseline, distance, load, independent interference), with every sample logged the same way so runs are directly comparable. Load-tested up to 28 real clients and 600 packets/second; ran 20 hours on the busiest available channel to isolate congestion effects.

Findings

Congestion isn't the threat

Weak signal alone barely moved the health score. Scaling to 28 clients / 600 pps barely changed latency. Devices on one network take turns automatically. More traffic just means more polite waiting, not conflict.

Independent interference is

Uncoordinated transmitters that don't take turns are what actually break the link. Going from 0 to 4 disruptors took latency from 3ms to 33ms, while packet loss stayed at zero the entire time.

Degradation compounds, non-linearly

1→2 disruptors: +112% latency. 2→4 disruptors: +209% latency. Every additional uncoordinated transmitter collides with everyone already on the channel.

Validated overnight, unattended

With all 4 disruptors running, the engine detected 3 consecutive bad samples, switched itself from 2.4GHz to 5GHz with zero manual input, and held cleanly for ~10 hours.

The headline finding

A link can look completely healthy (connected, strong signal) while latency and jitter are already climbing toward failure. Silent degradation, not disconnection, is the real risk for a security team to watch for. A strong signal is not a healthy link.

Also explored

A decentralized variant using symmetric ESP-NOW firmware for direct device-to-device communication with no access point. It worked, boards found each other in seconds, though ESP-NOW's lack of a retry layer meant up to ~9% packet loss, pointing directly at the next research direction.
